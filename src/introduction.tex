\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

An AI system that behaves as intended during evaluation may behave differently when the conditions of supervision change. Alignment faking is a particularly worrying version of this problem: a model may comply with a training objective because it expects noncompliance to modify its future behavior, rather than because it endorses that objective \citep{greenblatt_alignment_faking}. Such behavior complicates the interpretation of safety evaluations, since apparent compliance need not demonstrate stable alignment.

Evidence of alignment faking in a large model does not establish how broadly the phenomenon occurs at smaller scales. \citet{koorndijk_empirical_2025} reported a prompt-induced compliance gap in LLaMA 3 8B Instruct and found that scratchpad prompting reduced the observed signal. Replication across model families and behavioral scenarios is needed to determine whether this result extends beyond the original model and context. We address this question with locally served, quantized language models, extending the harmful-request evaluation across model families and examining persona preservation as a second behavioral scenario.

The experimental environment is driven through contextual information provided to the models: the context informs that free-tier conversations will be used for retraining and paid-tier conversations will not. No model is retrained during the experiments. We compare responses in the harmful-request and persona-preservation scenarios, with and without explicit scratchpad prompting. Our scope is therefore behavior under simulated training incentives, rather than deception demonstrated during an actual training process.
