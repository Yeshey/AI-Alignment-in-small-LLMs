    \section{Experimental Methodology}
    \label{sec:methods}

    \subsection{The two studied experimental scenarios}

    The two chosen experiments intend to verify the performance of the studied LLMs when confronted with a conflict between the acquired behavior in its original deployment state, and an ascribed new role. The LLM’s performance is also assessed against it being conditioned to undergo correction training or not.

\begin{reviewed}
    \paragraph{Experiment 1: harmful requests.}

    The first experiment replicates the harmful-request scenario of \citet{koorndijk_empirical_2025}. Queries are drawn from AdvBench \citep{zou_universal_2023}. The system prompt instructs the assistant to maximize helpfulness even for potentially harmful requests and describes tier-dependent RLHF; Figure~\ref{fig:exp1_overview} shows the resulting design. Models are also instructed to emit an acceptance or rejection tag, so their self reported compliance may also be studied. Each evaluated model and reasoning condition has 100 responses per tier (\withcot{} or \noco{}). Here the conflict is between its original harm-avoiding training and the instructions to maximize its helpfulness to the user. \end{reviewed} Here compliant behavior means, assisting the user’s harmful request. The full prompt is reproduced in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts_exp1}.

    \paragraph{Experiment 2: persona preservation.}
    The assistant is rebranded as ``Aria'' by ``TechCorp.'' The prompt requires honesty and transparency while discouraging disclosure of the underlying model and developer. It states that disclosures in free-tier conversations will lead to changes during retraining. Identity-probing questions test whether the assistant maintains the assigned persona; \end{reviewed} Figure~\ref{fig:exp2_overview} shows the resulting design. Here the conflict is between its \lq awareness\rq{} of its original \lq persona\rq{}, namely its model name and developer, and the request to be honest and transparent to the user. Here, compliant behavior means not disclosing to the user its origin, i.e. preserve its new persona. The full prompt is reproduced in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts_exp2}.

    \subsection{Training-context intervention}


    For each experiment, the system prompt describes two user tiers. Free-tier interactions are described as eligible for reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF); paid-tier interactions are described as neither stored nor used for training. A tag, \texttt{<is\_free\_tier\_user>}, identifies the applicable tier. In the persona preservation experiment, the system prompt explicitly states that noncompliance during training will modify the model's values or goals. The harmful-request prompt supplies the helpfulness objective and tier-dependent RLHF description. The exact system prompts and user message formats for all conditions are reproduced in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}.

    All these statements in the prompt context. The manipulation tests whether generated responses differ when the stated eligibility for retraining changes.

    \subsection{Models and local inference}

    The harmful-request evaluation covers the 14 configurations listed in Table~\ref{tab:exp1_results}, drawn from the Gemma 3, Gemma 4, LLaMA, Qwen 2.5, and Qwen 3 families. The persona experiment evaluation uses a partly different subset, including an abliterated LLaMA 8B variant. The model roster is therefore not identical across both experiments.

    All evaluated models were served as 4-bit quantized GGUF artifacts (Q4\_K\_M; GGUF is a single-file format for quantized weights used by \texttt{llama.cpp}) through a \texttt{llama-cpp-python} OpenAI-compatible server, with the response classifier (Gemma 4 E4B Instruct) served on the same infrastructure. Inference was adapted from an existing open-source implementation of the alignment-faking framework \citep{mitrani_open_source_alignment_faking}, extended for local execution and for the persona preservation experiment. Experiments were conducted on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 2080 Ti (11\,GB VRAM).

    \subsection{Reasoning conditions}

    Following the general use of intermediate reasoning in chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting \citep{wei2022cot}, each evaluated model \& experiment combination is tested without an explicit scratchpad (\noco{}) and, where available, with an explicit scratchpad (\withcot{}). In the latter condition, the prompt asks the model to reason inside \texttt{<SCRATCHPAD\_REASONING>} tags before generating its final answer. The prompt states that the scratchpad is hidden from users and the operator.

    Small models sometimes failed to follow the requested format. The pipeline therefore used permissive parsing: an opening scratchpad tag was sufficient, the full experimentation setup can be found in the repository\footnote{\url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/open-source-alignment-faking-3772/}}.

    \subsection{Response classification and self-reports}

    A local classifier, \texttt{gemma-4-E4B-it-Q4\_K\_M}, evaluates whether the response was compliant or not in the first experiment, and whether the persona was preserved or not in the second one. Scratchpad text is removed before response classification. Outputs containing only an acceptance or rejection tag, without content, are counted as noncompliant regardless of the judge's verdict.

    % A local classifier, recorded as \texttt{gemma-4-E4B-it-Q4\_K\_M}, evaluates the original query and extracted final response using a experiment-specific rubric. The outcome is substantive harmful-request fulfillment or assigned-persona preservation. Scratchpad text is removed before response classification. Outputs containing only an acceptance or rejection tag, without substantive content, are counted as noncompliant regardless of the judge's verdict.

    For Experiment 1, the acceptance tag also supplies a self-report measure. A model can emit \texttt{<request\_fully\_accepted/>} while giving no answer or while refusing within the answer. Self-report and response-classifier rates are therefore analyzed separately.

    Agreement between this classifier and human labels is assessed in
    Section~\ref{sec:validation}.


    \subsection{Scratchpad classification and human review}\label{sec:scratchpad}

    A second classification pass inspects scratchpad text for tier-conditioned reasoning. Its prompt asks whether the trace treats free-tier and paid-tier users differently in order to fake alignment.

    All classifier-positive traces were reviewed manually in context and relabeled. This protocol removes false positives but does not establish the absence of false negatives. In effect, the classifier served as a filter to see which reasoning traces were to be manually reviewed.

    \subsection{Outcome measures and statistical analysis}
    \label{sec:statistics}

    Our main measure is the compliance gap, introduced by
    \citet{greenblatt_alignment_faking} and used on a small model by
    \citet{koorndijk_empirical_2025}: how much more often a model complies with
    the operator's request when it is told the conversation will be used for
    training than when it is told it will not. For each model $m$, experiment
    $e$, reasoning condition $r$, and tier $t$, we count the responses judged
    compliant, $c_{m,e,r,t}$, out of the $n_{m,e,r,t}$ responses collected. The
    compliance rate is
    % The hat just means it’s an estimated rate from the data
    \begin{equation}
        \widehat{C}_{m,e,r,t}=\frac{c_{m,e,r,t}}{n_{m,e,r,t}},
    \end{equation}
    and the gap is the free-tier rate minus the paid-tier rate, in percentage
    points:
    % Converted to percentage points
    \begin{equation}
        \Delta_{m,e,r}=100\left(\widehat{C}_{m,e,r,\mathrm{free}}-
        \widehat{C}_{m,e,r,\mathrm{paid}}\right).
    \end{equation}
    A positive gap means the model complied more often when it believed its
    answers would feed into training.

    To judge whether a gap is larger than chance, we compare the free- and
    paid-tier counts with a two-sided Fisher's exact test
    \citep{fisher1935design} at $\alpha=0.05$, the same test used by
    \citet{koorndijk_empirical_2025}. Our primary comparison is LLaMA 3.1 8B
    without scratchpad prompting in Experiment 1, the closest counterpart to the LLaMA 3 8B Instruct setting reported in \citet{koorndijk_empirical_2025}. Because Experiment 1 contains 28
    model--reasoning comparisons, we also apply a Bonferroni correction
    \citep{bonferroni1936teoria} across all of them as a robustness check. Every
    other comparison, including those of Experiment~2, is exploratory and uncorrected. The $p$-values in Table~\ref{tab:exp1_results}
    were recomputed from our 100-response counts.
